Fit to Serve

Creative advocacy · Fashion design · Ergonomics

Standard military uniforms were designed around a male body. Women have worn them anyway, adjusting, making do and putting up with the discomfort. Fit to Serve redesigns the uniform with women’s bodies, movements and needs as the starting point instead of an afterthought. It is a design project, and it is also an argument: being included in the design means being recognized as someone worth designing for.

The project has two parts that go together: a working prototype of a redesigned women’s uniform, and Unspoken Valor, a book of women veterans’ own stories that puts their voices at the center of the design. Both were shown and sold at a pop-up exhibition at DMZ, a military apparel shop in Itaewon, Seoul.

Why I made it

I came to this through bioengineering, and a belief that science can improve people’s lives. But the further I went into science, the more I ran into its limits. Science solves what it can measure. Suffering that has no language, experiences that resist numbers, and people whose lives fall outside the data are too often left at the edges of the systems meant to serve them.

Prosthetic limbs came from engineers designing for people conventional systems overlooked, and art has long stood beside marginalized communities, making the unheard audible and the unseen visible. Fit to Serve sits where the two meet: using the problem-solving of engineering and the language of art to give form to experiences that usually go unseen.

The problem

Talking with women veterans, I learned that wearing uniforms designed for men caused problems ranging from small daily discomforts to real limits on how they could move and work. Each one seemed minor on its own. Together, they affected mobility, comfort and performance, showing how a gap in design turns into a gap in experience.

That led to a simple question: if a uniform could be designed around women’s bodies at roughly the same production cost, why should women keep adapting themselves to clothing that was never made for them?

Seven design modifications

Each change answers a specific problem the veterans described, in comfort, access, movement or carrying equipment.

1. Accessible lower-body zipper
A standard front zip is designed for male anatomy and isn’t practical for many women. A second, lower zipper makes restroom access and menstrual hygiene faster and easier, especially in demanding field conditions.
2. Diagonal upper-body closure
A straight front closure presses directly against the chest. The jacket fastens diagonally instead, following the contours of the chest and taking pressure off the upper torso.
3. Load-distributing harness and leg pocket
Women tend to outperform men in endurance more than in strength-based tasks; in mixed-gender 200 km races, women often come out ahead. A multi-pocket harness moves load that usually sits on the shoulders down toward the hips and lower body, easing upper-body strain over long periods of wear. An extra leg pocket adds storage that is easy to reach.
4. Adjustable knee ventilation
Women hold and release more heat through the lower body. Vented openings at the knees, closed with Velcro, let the wearer adjust airflow to the weather and how hard they are working, cutting heat buildup without giving up protection.
5. Adjustable ankle straps
Women with smaller frames often end up with extra fabric at the hem, especially when switching between combat boots, insulated boots and other field footwear. Adjustable straps at the cuffs fit the hem to the leg and the boot, keeping movement clean and cold out.
6. Modular equipment attachment points
Velcro attachment points across the chest and shoulders let equipment be fixed to the body wherever it is needed. That means less reaching and lifting with the arms, and less fatigue during tasks that need gear again and again.
7. Adjustable rear waistband
An adjustable elastic waistband at the back lets the wearer change the fit throughout the day, so the uniform moves with the body instead of shifting or bunching.

Rather than asking women to adapt to a uniform, the uniform should adapt to the women who wear it.

Fit to Serve × DMZ pop-up

DMZ is a military apparel shop in Itaewon that draws people interested in uniforms, military history and military-inspired fashion. Its owner has spent more than 35 years researching, collecting and archiving military uniforms, and that knowledge shaped the project in ways I couldn’t have managed alone, grounding each design decision in how military garments are actually built, used and worn.

Showing a redesigned women’s uniform in a shop full of standard ones asked visitors to look again at familiar clothing through ergonomics, gender and personal history. The pop-up displayed the prototype next to Unspoken Valor, so visitors could see both the design changes and the stories behind them, and offered both for sale to support the project’s next steps and future exhibitions.

What it’s for

  • Making women’s military experience visible. Bringing attention to the physical experiences, histories and overlooked needs of women who serve.
  • Reaching new audiences. Meeting DMZ’s regular customers as well as people drawn in by fashion, art and inclusive design.
  • Treating the uniform as a statement. Presenting military clothing not just as standard equipment, but as an object that carries questions of identity, representation and access.

Design is never neutral: every object reflects assumptions about who it is meant to serve. Fit to Serve tries to make those assumptions visible, and to offer a more inclusive alternative.
