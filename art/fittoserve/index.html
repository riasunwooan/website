Fit to Serve

Creative advocacy · Fashion design · Ergonomics

Standard military uniforms were designed around a male body. Women have worn them anyway, adjusting, making do and putting up with the discomfort. Fit to Serve redesigns the uniform from the ground up, treating women’s bodies, movements and needs as the starting point instead of an afterthought. It is a design project, and it is also an argument: being included in the design means being recognized as someone worth designing for.

The project grew out of the same interviews with women veterans behind Unspoken Valor.

The problem

Talking with women veterans, I learned that uniforms designed for male bodies cause women problems ranging from minor discomfort to real losses in performance. The more I listened, the clearer it became that these weren’t small complaints. When the environment doesn’t account for women, it holds them back. So I asked whether the uniform could be redesigned for a woman’s body at roughly the same cost, without starting from scratch.

Design modifications

Lower zip for biological needs
The standard front zip was designed for male anatomy, and women don’t use it the same way. A second zip lower down gives faster, easier access during menstruation and for basic biological needs, cutting out time and friction that shouldn’t be there in the first place.
Diagonal closure
A straight front closure presses directly against the chest. The redesigned top fastens diagonally, which reduces chest compression and fits a woman’s chest more naturally.
Weight-redistribution harness
Research shows women tend to outperform men in endurance more than in strength-based tasks; in mixed-gender 200 km races, women often come out ahead. So the design shifts load off the upper body and down through a multi-pocket harness, with an extra leg pocket that moves gear weight toward the lower body, where women’s physiology is strongest.
Knee ventilation panels
Women hold and release more heat through the lower body. Velcro-secured vents at the knee let the wearer adjust airflow to the conditions, cutting heat buildup without giving up protection.
Adjustable ankle straps
Women soldiers, especially those with smaller frames, often struggle with excess fabric at the hem when they switch between combat, cold-weather and field boots. Adjustable ankle straps tighten or loosen the hem as needed, so movement is cleaner and less cold gets in during field operations.
Chest and shoulder Velcro points
To rely less on arm strength, Velcro panels across the chest and shoulders let equipment attach directly to the body at any point. That means less lifting, less adjusting and less fatigue.
Rear waistband adjustment
An adjustable rear waistband lets the wearer change the fit at the waist throughout the day, so clothing doesn’t shift or bunch during long wear.

Fit to Serve × DMZ pop-up

With the owner of DMZ, a military surplus and clothing store in Itaewon, Seoul, I organized a pop-up exhibition and sale featuring the redesigned uniform. The owner has spent more than 35 years researching, collecting and archiving military uniforms and their history, and that knowledge shaped the project in ways I couldn’t have managed alone, grounding each design decision in how military garments are actually built, used and worn.

The store became a place to show both the garment and the thinking behind it: why it was made, who it was made for, and what it means to design with a person in mind rather than around them. Alongside the uniform, I exhibited and sold Unspoken Valor, which carries the voices of the women veterans I spoke with: the frustrations they had come to accept, the adjustments they had quietly made for years, and what it felt like to finally be considered in the design of something they wear every day.
