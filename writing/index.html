CL Writing About Art · Nov 2026

How Art Does: An Unfinished Theory

    Before you begin

    Find a piece of artwork, reflect on it, and answer the five questions. There are two answers, Yes or No, to each question, much like a choose-your-own-adventure. To have a personal experience, follow your chosen path through the guide without reading the answers you didn’t select. To find my own ongoing, rather chaotic attempt to understand what art does to us, read every path as one continuous essay.

    There are no wrong answers. I’m increasingly uncertain there are right ones, either.

    Ria An · Mr. Taylor · CL Sr English Seminar: Writing About Art

    Do you like this artwork?

    How lovely. But what, exactly, have you fallen for?

    As someone who has been dragged around the Sistine Chapel, Santa Maria delle Grazie, Peggy Guggenheim Collection, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, MoMA, and many more museums to encounter some of the world’s most renowned masterpieces, I never expected to find a painting worthy of my computer wallpaper in Hartford, CT.

    Yet there I was, plopped on the floor of the Wadsworth Atheneum, gazing up at the colossal scene of ancient ruins beneath a snow-capped volcano. I knew neither its title nor its artist. I only knew I liked it. During our first museum visit, it was the one painting that stopped me in my tracks, holding me in a trance. Admittedly, I had always been a bit skeptical of the supposedly visceral encounters with art I had read about, sympathizing with Ben Lerner as he worried that he was “incapable of having a profound experience of art.” Reading his Leaving the Atocha Station, I scoffed at the theatrical notion that a painting could have someone sobbing. My instinct, after all those curators that droned on endlessly about art history in all my past museum visits, was to analyze: identify the artistic movement, investigate the painter, dissect the composition, and find the symbolisms. But nope, even after scouring the museum for a piece that might surpass it during our second visit, I found myself returning to the same canvas.

    But why? Perhaps its size? Well, I’ve stood before Michelangelo’s monumental The Last Judgement before. Its detail? But my eyes have traced the myriad of paint layers in Pollock’s Autumn Rhythm: Number 30, 1950 in the Met. Its beauty? Surely it can’t surpass the luminous colors of Monet’s Water Lilies. None of these explanations seemed sufficient.

    I realized, though, that I had begun imagining stories beholding the painting. I was melting into another world, where voices used to ring in those pillars, where the mountain rumbled, witnessing civilizations rising and falling, where anything could happen (maybe a dragon flew over, roaring and breathing fire). As an only child, and the youngest in a family where everyone was an adult by the time I was six, I grew up without playmates, but instead, with countless worlds. Books, films, art, conversations—anything was a doorway. And this painting, suspended between destruction and rebirth, invited me to imagine. So, ultimately, I was looking at myself.

    The painting: Thomas Cole, Mount Etna from Taormina, Wadsworth Atheneum →

    How unfortunate. Or perhaps, how fortunate. Must art please us to succeed?

    The Cambridge Dictionary says, “art” is the making of objects, images, music, etc. that are beautiful or that express feelings. Notice the “or.”

    I certainly did not like Munch’s The Scream, because it vibrated with an anxiety I wanted nothing to do with, literally making me want to scream back. Yet it is praised as a masterpiece. In Ucky, Elkin explores the parallel of my reaction to Munch’s work, examining Eva Hesse’s unsettling sculptures, works that resist the comfort of recognizable forms and familiar materials. Their strangeness is what kept her looking; perhaps it was also why her encounter with Hesse’s artwork was so meaningful. Reading this, I began to wonder whether my discomfort came not from the artwork’s failure in its first half of the definition of “beautiful,” but from its success in expressing feelings I was unaccustomed to confronting.

    A mirror does not flatter by returning what we want to recognize. Art is a mirror of humanity, and art, too, need not be so polite.

    Is this artwork “good?”

    (artistic value?)

    You have recognized artistic merit. But whose eyes taught you to recognize it?

    Consider Picasso’s Les Demoiselles d’Avignon. Five women confront the viewer through fractured bodies, distorted proportions, and faces that do not follow the conventions of classical beauty. Nowadays, the painting is celebrated as a turning point in modern art. Yet its revolutionary significance was hardly self-evident to those who first encountered it. What now appears as pioneering once appeared almost incomprehensible.

    Claire Bishop’s Disordered Attention reminds us that the conditions of spectatorship influence how we perceive art. A painting does not change when it enters a museum, but its authority does. Everyone approaches the same canvas differently when it has the label “masterpiece.” Perhaps artistic greatness is not simply discovered but gradually constructed through the judgements of critics, historians, and institutions. We like to believe that artistic value possesses some universal language requiring no translation, yet what we call “good taste” is merely a dialect we have to learn in order to speak fluently. So, I may have judged the artwork. But how much of that judgement belongs to me?

    Since I can remember, there have been two huge Rothko prints in our living room. Just rectangles of color, their edges dissolving into one another. No figures, no landscapes, no discernible subjects. I never quite understood them, yet I never questioned whether they were art. They simply were art, as naturally as the sofa beneath them was furniture.

    But clearly, someone designed the shape of the sofa, selected its fabric, and perhaps stitched its upholstery by hand. Why did one object invite artistic contemplation while the other was dismissed as domestic utility? Because to me, if a canvas colored in three colors is called “grand artwork,” a carefully designed sofa (or intricately embroidered quilt, as Elkin uses as an example), should be art instead of craft.

    In Ucky, Elkin examines how artists like Eva Hesse challenged the boundaries between sculpture and materials traditionally associated with domestic labor. Forms of handiwork associated with women have often been denied comparable recognition compared to painting and sculpture, which have occupied the prestigious territory of art. But what we dismiss as craft is not less creative, only the kind of creativity we have learned to overlook.

    When Mr. Taylor introduced Sol LeWitt to our class, whose conceptual wall drawing was executed entirely by assistants following his written instructions (who didn’t even receive credit, by the way), our class was outraged. I, likewise, refused to accept that his work was art. Because, if the absence of physical labor can elevate an idea into fine art, what distinguished LeWitt’s instructions from a prompt entered into an AI image generator?

    In an attempt to defend my opinion here, I returned, rather desperately, to the dictionary. Art is defined as the making of objects, images, music, etc. that are beautiful or that express feelings. So, technically, LeWitt’s work and AI-created work would qualify as art. Yet I still felt the conflict was unresolved in my gut. The dictionary told me what an artwork might be, not why I was willing to recognize one creator and dismiss another. I realized I may have been asking insufficient questions. Perhaps I should’ve asked not merely what makes something art, but who gets to be called an artist. And so, I turned to the people behind the work.

    Read the artist’s biography. Does it change your mind about Q2?

    How quickly a life becomes an easy explanation. How comforting, too, to believe that every artistic mystery has a biographical key. Because we humans hate not knowing the answer.

    Consider Artemisia Gentileschi’s Judith Beheading Holofernes. In class, when I first saw this painting without any background knowledge, I wondered if the painter was a monstrous man who was scared of this happening in the future, or, as my clichéd instincts suggested, a woman expressing her personal anguish. To be honest, I hoped it wasn’t a woman who was raped. While I was busy imagining biographies, Will, seated next to me, remarked that he loved the painting’s composition and technique. I stayed silent to keep down the bile rising in my throat imagining yet another woman’s desperation and pain from sexual violence, desperately using the canvas as an outlet to her anguish in a society that denied her justice.

    And once I learned about the violence Gentileschi experienced, suddenly, the biblical painting became an autobiography. But should her artistic imagination be confined to her suffering? Society celebrates Caravaggio’s dramatic chiaroscuro as artistic mastery, but Gentileschi’s equally deliberate composition and skills that Will was able to appreciate as an emotional confession. In my eagerness to understand the woman, have I forgotten the painter? Or did the painting become more powerful because it came from the depths of her heart?

    Biography can also work in reverse. In What Do We Do with the Art of Monstrous Men?, Claire Dederer recounts researching Roman Polanski’s sexual assault of a minor, only to discover that she remained captivated by his films. The knowledge, instead of erasing the film’s artistic power, morphed her artistic appreciation into a source of discomfort.

    Let’s return to Picasso’s Les Demoiselles d’Avignon. Those five fractured female bodies we previously admired as a revolutionary rejection of stereotypical beauty acquire another uncomfortable dimension when we learn about his misogyny and abuse of women. Are we still looking at an audacious artistic experiment, or at women distorted through the gaze of a monster who used and discarded women as objects? I have to agree with Dederer, “The awful thing disrupts the great work.”

    I find this contradiction peculiar. We may elevate Gentileschi’s painting because we sympathize with its creator, yet diminish Picasso’s because we condemn its creator. Inevitably, artists leave pieces of themselves in their work.

    Biography is most seductive when it offers the satisfaction of certainty. I remember telling my parents how I don’t like how Picasso’s paintings make me feel even before I knew about his biography. Now, knowing more about his treatment of women, I find myself tempted to interpret that discomfort as an intuition I couldn’t yet explain. How satisfying, to discover that I was right all along, and how disgusting, that society idolizes these paintings, because in a way, we are celebrating the abuse the women went through. So, I still hesitate, unable to stop looking nor stop picturing his paintings. As I don’t want to dismiss his painting because I condemn the man, and don’t want to pretend the man disappears the moment I look at the paintings, either, I’m beginning to think the responsibility lies with us.

    Museums, critics, society, you and I, are all responsible to admire the brushstrokes while questioning the hands that made them, remembering that the women were not merely subjects on a canvas but people with stories of their own.

    Wow, you must be admirably impartial. You have decided to let the art speak for itself.

    Did you really, though? If I haven’t watched the interviews from Creative Growth Art Center, I might’ve walked past Judith Scott’s artwork without giving them much thought. Although it pains me in my moral conscience, I wonder: would these sculptures be as celebrated if it wasn’t created by her? If I had assembled the same materials into the exact same forms, would they hang in a museum? Would critics call them extraordinary? Would I?

    It troubles me that I’m even asking this question. As the director of Creative Growth argues in the Art21 documentary, Scott and her fellow artists deserve to be recognized alongside other contemporary artists, “not [in] a pavilion of disability, not a pavilion of the self-taught.” Their work should be celebrated for what it contributes to art, not confined to a category defined by their disabilities. He goes further, calling the denial of creative opportunities to people with disabilities a “human rights issue.”

    Perhaps this is precisely the distinction I’ve been missing. Scott’s biography might not tell me whether her sculptures are good (you are one step ahead of me, since you didn’t let the biography change your opinion on the artwork’s quality), but it does tell me about the barriers artists face from the institutions that decide what “good” art looks like. And as I have been raised in those institutions, their definitions of artistic merit have become my native language. Even when I try to challenge them to speak another tongue, I find myself thinking in their grammar.

    Do you like this artwork?

    The artwork hasn’t changed. Have you?

    Who told you there had to be an answer?

    Close this guide and open your eyes. As Sal Randolph said, a painting is nothing without you.

    “The painting, in this moment, needs you, exactly you. Nothing less.”
