Day 1 Sun 5/5/24

  • Researched current depression screening methods and their limitations
  • Collected data on the depression status quo
  • Global
    • One in seven 10–19-year-olds experiences a mental disorder, 13% of the global burden of disease in this age group
    • Depression, anxiety and behavioural disorders are among the leading causes of illness and disability among adolescents
    • Suicide is the fourth leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds
  • United States
    • About 1 in 5 adolescents report symptoms of anxiety or depression (KFF); many don’t get therapy because of cost, fear of what others will think, or not knowing how to get help
    • In 2021, 20% of 12–17-year-olds had at least one major depressive episode (MDE): 29% of females vs 12% of males; nearly 60% received no treatment
    • Teens who recently experienced depression rose 59% between 2007 and 2017 (66% for girls, 44% for boys)
    • Seven in ten teens call anxiety and depression a major problem among people their age (Pew Research Center, 2018); another 26% call it a minor problem
    • NIMH: 17% of 12–17-year-olds had a major depressive episode in 2020 (4.1 million teens); 12% had one with severe impairment
    • CDC (2021): 42% of students felt persistently sad or hopeless, 29% had poor mental health, 22% considered suicide, 10% attempted suicide
    • 4.8 million youth (19.5%) have major depression and 3.6 million (14.6%) severe depression; 59.8% of youth with depression received no mental health treatment
    • Adolescent depression rose from 8.1% in 2009 to 15.8% in 2019
    • MDD is the leading cause of disability for ages 15–44, affects about 8.4% of U.S. adults, and almost 49% of adults with MDD are untreated
    • 80–90% of people with depression who seek treatment eventually respond well to it
  • South Korea
    • In 2021, South Korea’s suicide rate was 26 per 100,000, the highest in the OECD; suicide was the main cause of death for ages 10–39 (44% of teenage deaths, 56.8% of deaths in people in their 20s), with mental illness the leading cause
    • Of about 50,000 middle and high school students surveyed in 2022, 14.3% had thought about suicide in the past 12 months
    • Suicide has been the number one cause of death for young people since 2007

How depression is diagnosed today. A doctor has to hear about specific symptoms, usually through standard screening questions. To be diagnosed, a person must have symptoms most of the day, nearly every day, for at least 2 weeks, including a depressed mood or loss of interest. Children and adolescents may be irritable rather than sad, and depression can look very different from person to person (withdrawal, irritability, changes in eating and sleep), which makes diagnosis hard.

Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI)

  • Modified from the adult Beck Depression Inventory
  • Ages 7–17; two scales: emotional and functional problems
  • Forms for parents (17 items), teachers (12) and a self-report (28)
  • 5–15 minutes

Children’s Depression Rating Scale (CDRS)

  • Designed for ages 6–12, validated up to 18
  • 17 symptom areas, adapted from the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale
  • Semi-structured interview
  • 10–15 minutes to give and score

Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9)

  • Self-report screen for depressive symptoms
  • 1–5 minutes to complete, about the same to review
  • Available in many languages
  • 61% sensitivity / 94% specificity in adults; 89.5% / 77.5% in adolescents
  • Common thread: every existing method is a self-report questionnaire, which only works if the person recognizes their symptoms and answers honestly

Day 6 Sun 6/9/24

  • Background study on GSR and EEG

Galvanic Skin Response (GSR)

  • What it is: the electrical conductivity of the skin in response to stimuli; emotional triggers, positive or negative, activate sweat glands and raise conductivity
  • Why it’s useful: these changes are involuntary (autonomic nervous system), so GSR detects emotional intensity even when someone tries to hide it
  • Measurement: conductance between two points on the skin, in microsiemens (µS); higher conductance = more sweat-gland activity = higher arousal
  • Device: sensors on the index and middle fingers with Velcro, connected by Bluetooth; participants sit still, breathe normally and don’t talk
  • Signal: a slow-varying tonic level (baseline) plus fast phasic responses (peaks) 1–5 seconds after an emotional trigger
  • Analysis: count the peaks over time; more peaks = higher arousal

Electroencephalography (EEG)

  • What it is: a non-invasive way to record the brain’s electrical activity with electrodes on the scalp
  • Mechanism: neurons exchange ions across their membranes; the charge spreads by volume conduction, and electrodes pick up the voltage differences, which are amplified and graphed
  • Clinical use: spotting abnormal brain waves, e.g., seizures appear as rapid spiking waves
  • International 10-20 system: standard electrode placement; letters mark the lobe (Fp, F, T, P, O, C, A), even numbers = right side, odd = left, “z” = midline
  • Why combine them: EEG is very sensitive to noise, and GSR shows the strength of emotion the person feels unconsciously, so it can compensate

Day 7 Thu 6/13/24

  • Gathered background knowledge on EEG datasets
  • Read: Liu, Qiu, Zheng & Lu (2021), comparing recognition performance and robustness of multimodal deep learning models for emotion recognition (IEEE Transactions on Cognitive and Developmental Systems)
  • Chosen: SEED-IV (BCMI lab)
    • 72 film clips, chosen in a preliminary study to tend to induce happiness, sadness, fear or neutral emotions

Day 8 Mon 6/17/24

  • Chosen: DEAP
    • Multimodal dataset for analyzing human affective states
    • EEG and GSR from 32 participants, each watching 40 one-minute music-video excerpts

Day 9 Sat 6/22/24

  • Researched the arousal-valence (A-V) model
  • Two dimensions
    • Arousal: physiological and psychological activation, from low (calm, relaxed) to high (excitement, agitation)
    • Valence: how positive or negative the emotion is, from negative (sadness, anger) to positive (happiness, delight)
  • Four quadrants
    • High arousal, positive valence: excitement, surprise
    • High arousal, negative valence: anger, fear, anxiety
    • Low arousal, positive valence: calmness, relaxation
    • Low arousal, negative valence: sadness, depression, boredom
  • Why it matters: depression sits in the low-arousal, negative-valence quadrant, so predicting a point on this graph is a way to screen for it without asking any questions

Day 15 Tue 7/16/24

  • Built the TOP-N model component
  • Phase 1: representation learning
    • EEG signals are converted into topographical maps, one map per epoch
    • An unsupervised CNN learns emotion-related feature vectors without labels
    • Assumption: feature vectors from similar emotions should be mathematically similar, enforced with the cosine-similarity loss (Eq. 1)
  • Phase 2: transfer learning
    • The pre-trained TOP-N becomes the feature extractor
    • E-RN is trained on top to predict arousal and valence, using MSE (Eq. 2)
