An Ethical Three-Layer Framework for Evaluating AI Responses to Mental Health Crises

Jul 2026 – present · Stony Brook University

Peer reviewers Ryn Linthicum, Head of User Well-Being Policy at Anthropic & Dr. Nicola Hohensee, Postdoctoral Associate in the Child Study Center, Yale University

As I watched generative AI become a place where young people increasingly disclose thoughts they may never share with another person and discussed this with the VOICE Youth Advisory Board, I became interested in what an AI system does and should do when the user is in crisis. Reading existing research, I found that answers were often separated by discipline: empirical studies examined what chatbots actually say, philosophers debated what they should say, and policy analyses examined the systems behind those responses. Thus, I developed a three-layer framework that brings these perspectives together, evaluating AI at the interaction, principle, and structural levels. Drawing on empirical evidence, five philosophical traditions, incentive analysis, and three real-world case studies, I examine not only when an AI response fails, but where that failure originates, and thus where and how an intervention needs to occur. This project has pushed me to think beyond whether a technology “works” and ask how its design, values, and incentives together determine whom it ultimately serves, and to make sure innovation doesn’t leave humanity behind.

The three layers

Interaction
What the AI actually says to a user in crisis.
Principle
Whether that response holds up across five philosophical traditions.
Structure
The commercial incentives that shape how the system was built to respond.
