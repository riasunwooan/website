Day 1 Tue 5/6/25

  • Collected background data on youth mental health from last year’s research logbook and online sources
  • Global picture
    • One in seven 10–19-year-olds experiences a mental disorder, accounting for 13% of the global burden of disease in this age group
    • Depression, anxiety and behavioural disorders are among the leading causes of illness and disability among adolescents
    • Suicide is the fourth leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds
  • United States
    • About 1 in 5 adolescents report symptoms of anxiety or depression (KFF analysis of a federal survey of teen health); many say they don’t get therapy because of cost, stigma, or not knowing how to get help
    • In 2021, 20% of 12–17-year-olds had at least one major depressive episode (MDE); prevalence among females (29%) was more than twice that of males (12%), and nearly 60% received no treatment
    • Teens who recently experienced depression rose 59% between 2007 and 2017 (66% for girls, 44% for boys)
    • Seven in ten U.S. teens call anxiety and depression a major problem among people their age (Pew Research Center, 2018)
    • NIMH: 17% of 12–17-year-olds had a major depressive episode in 2020 (4.1 million teens); 12% had at least one episode with severe impairment
    • CDC (2021): 42% of students felt persistently sad or hopeless, 29% experienced poor mental health, 22% considered suicide and 10% attempted suicide
    • Adolescent depression rose from 8.1% in 2009 to 15.8% in 2019
    • MDD is the leading cause of disability in the U.S. for ages 15–44, affects about 8.4% of adults, and almost half of adults with MDD are untreated
  • South Korea
    • Of about 50,000 middle and high school students surveyed in 2022, 14.3% had thought about suicide in the past 12 months (Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency)

Key takeaway. Today, 1 in 6 children ages 6–17 experiences a mental health disorder each year, a 35% increase since 2016. Yet over 80% of these children never receive care from a mental health provider, and only about one-third of children with mental health challenges are identified in primary care settings.

Doctors alone cannot catch most cases. If clinics cannot do it alone, schools must play a larger role: not as diagnostic centers, but as early-warning systems.

Day 2 Mon 5/12/25

  • Researched HTP logic: what each drawing and feature is thought to reflect
  • Each drawing maps to a domain
    • House → home life, family dynamics, sense of safety
    • Tree → sense of self, emotional stability, growth
    • Person → self-image, identity, interpersonal relationships

House

  • Overall size
    • Very small → insecurity, withdrawal
    • Very large → need for attention, compensation
  • Door
    • Large/open → openness, accessibility
    • Very small or no door → guardedness, isolation
  • Windows
    • Many/open → social openness
    • No windows → emotional withdrawal
  • Chimney / smoke
    • Smoke present → warmth, family interaction
    • Excessive or chaotic smoke → emotional tension
  • Walls
    • Heavy/dark lines → defensiveness
    • Weak/fragmented → fragile boundaries
  • Ground line
    • Floating house → instability
    • Firm grounding → stability

Tree

  • Trunk
    • Thick → strength, ego stability
    • Thin → vulnerability
    • Broken → trauma indicators
  • Roots
    • Visible roots → need for security
    • No roots → possible instability
  • Crown
    • Large/full → imagination, emotional richness
    • Very small → limited expression
  • Branches
    • Upward → optimism
    • Downward → sadness, fatigue
    • Broken → emotional distress
  • Fruit
    • Often linked to productivity or a need for validation

Person

  • Overall size
    • Very small → low self-esteem
    • Very large → dominance, compensation
  • Head
    • Very large → intellectual focus
    • Very small → feelings of inadequacy
  • Eyes
    • Large/detailed → hypervigilance
    • Omitted → avoidance
  • Arms
    • Open arms → social engagement
    • No arms → helplessness
    • Very long → need for control
  • Legs
    • Strong/stable → confidence
    • Weak/missing → insecurity
  • Facial expression
    • Smiling → social conformity
    • No face → emotional suppression
  • Structural patterns clinicians may examine
    • Proportions (e.g., head-to-body ratio)
    • Symmetry
    • Line pressure
    • Feature omission
    • Feature exaggeration
    • Spatial placement
    • Relative size comparisons
  • Takeaway: most of these cues are about which parts are present, how big they are and where they sit. That is exactly what an object detector measures, so detection is a natural first step toward automated HTP analysis

Day 3 Tue 5/27/25

  • Studied Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)
  • The problem it solves: catastrophic forgetting
    • When a neural network is trained on a new task, the weights that mattered for the old task get overwritten, so performance on the old task collapses
    • This matters for HTP drawings: a model that first learns big objects (house, tree, person) could forget them while learning small parts (windows, fruit, eyes)
  • The idea
    • After training on an earlier task, record the parameter values θC and how important each parameter is to that task
    • While training on the next task, add a penalty that pulls important parameters back toward θC; unimportant parameters stay free to change
    • Introduced by Kirkpatrick et al. (2017) for continual learning

Day 4 Sat 5/31/25

  • Continued studying EWC
  • Notes on applying it to object detection
    • Treat coarse detection (whole house / tree / person) as the earlier task and fine-grained parts as the later task
    • The regularization strength (β) trades off remembering the coarse task against learning the new one: too small and the model forgets, too large and it can’t adapt
    • Related reading (cited on the poster): Kutalev & Lapina (2021), “Stabilizing Elastic Weight Consolidation method in practical ML tasks”

Day 6 Sun 6/8/25

  • Wrote down why an HTP-based system is specifically needed
  • Multi-dimensional psychological insight (cognitive + emotional + environmental)
    • The HTP test gives structured access to three core domains: House (family relationships and emotional security), Tree (vitality, physical self-image, inner strength) and Person (self-concept, social roles, personal identity)
    • By contrast, the “Person in the Rain” test focuses mainly on coping and vulnerability, a narrower window into psychological functioning
  • Well-validated interpretive framework
    • Decades of psychological research support HTP’s symbolic interpretations (e.g., missing limbs may suggest trauma, enclosed houses may suggest anxiety, rooted trees may reflect stability)
    • This lets the AI model be mapped to known psychological indicators, giving it stronger theoretical grounding than more abstract drawings like rain scenes
  • AI compatibility with discrete visual features
    • HTP figures have well-defined, object-like parts (windows, branches, hands) that suit object-recognition models such as YOLO
    • The system can quantify these cues, which is hard to do with more interpretive or dreamlike drawings
  • Cross-age applicability
    • Used with children, adolescents and adults, so the system is more age-flexible than verbal interviews or writing-based diagnostics
    • Requires no advanced verbal skills, so it suits language-minimal populations, including children with ASD, trauma or selective mutism
  • Lower bias and more cultural neutrality
    • Open verbal interviews can be affected by language fluency, cultural norms or clinician bias; an AI-HTP tool can standardize interpretation with a consistent visual-symbolic framework
  • Supports longitudinal monitoring
    • Drawings collected at multiple time points can track emotional changes over time, which is rarely feasible with situational tests like “Person in the Rain”

Day 8 Tue 6/17/25

  • Chose the dataset: the publicly available AI-Hub Child Psychological Drawing Image Dataset from the Korean government’s AI-Hub platform (AweHub 2025)
  • 56,000 drawings from children ages 7–13 with varied backgrounds in South Korea, collected and labeled specifically for training and evaluation
  • Annotations
    • Bounding boxes around meaningful visual components
    • Coarse-grained classes: house, tree, person
    • Fine-grained classes, e.g., trunk, fruit, crown for trees; window, smoke, door for houses
  • The dataset contains only legally compliant image data for object recognition (non-medical); it excludes any medical data involving psychological interpretation

Day 9 Fri 6/20/25

  • Identified three challenges for detecting objects in HTP drawings
    • Grayscale input: pencil sketches have no color, so the model must rely on edges, shape and texture
    • High domain variance: drawings vary widely with age, motor skill, culture and emotional expression, which makes them visually inconsistent
    • Fine-grained parts: windows, doors and smoke trails are often abstract, small and incomplete, so they are hard to localize and classify

Day 10 Fri 6/27/25

  • Plan to address these challenges
    • (1) Curriculum learning: organize training from simple to complex tasks
    • (2) Continual learning with EWC, so earlier skills aren’t forgotten
    • (3) Evaluate empirically to show gradual knowledge accumulation without forgetting
  • Curriculum stages (see Day 12 diagram)
    • Stage 1: big objects (easy samples)
    • Stage 2: middle-size objects
    • Stage 3: small objects (complex samples)
    • Parameters are updated stage by stage, with EWC protecting what was learned before

  • Experiment 4: statistical analysis, testing whether the model’s output can contribute meaningfully to psychological screening
