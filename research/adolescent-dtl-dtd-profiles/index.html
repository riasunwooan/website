Mapping the Internal Debate: Latent Profiles of Real-Time Dynamics in Desire to Live and Desire to Die in Adolescents

Feb 2026 – Jun 2026 · Cha Lab, Yale Child Study Center

Authors Olivia C. Lawrence; Ki Eun Shin, PhD; Layne Novotny; Ria Sunwoo An; Christine B. Cha, PhD

Key points

Profiles
Four real-time DTL–DTD motivational profiles identified in community adolescents
Risk
Low Life-Orientation profile showed elevated suicidal behavior despite low DTD
Novel
DTL–DTD coupling emerged as a novel distinguishing feature across profiles

Abstract

Objective

Recent efforts to identify subtypes of suicidal ideation have sought to improve the understanding and prevention of suicidal states. However, much of this work has focused on death-oriented ideation in adults, with limited attention to desire to live (DTL) and adolescents. This study aims to identify latent motivational profiles based on real-time DTL–desire to die (DTD) dynamics and examine whether they differed in lifetime suicidal ideation (SI) and suicidal behavior.

Method

Participants were 121 community adolescents aged 15–17 years who completed a 14-day ecological momentary assessment (EMA) protocol with five daily prompts assessing DTL and DTD. Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted using within-person means, maximum values, variability composites, and DTL–DTD coupling indices. The BCH three-step method examined profile differences in lifetime SI and suicidal behavior.

Results

Four motivational profiles emerged: Stable, Life-Oriented (75%), Moderately Ambivalent (14%), Low Life-Orientation (6%), and Highly Ambivalent (5%). Profiles differed in average motivational levels, temporal variability, and DTL–DTD coupling. Significant differences emerged in lifetime SI and suicidal behavior, with SI ranging from 31.9% to 100% and suicidal behavior from 4.4% to 42.9%. Notably, the Low Life-Orientation profile, characterized by diminished DTL rather than elevated DTD, showed the second-highest rate of suicidal behavior.

Conclusion

Modeling DTL and DTD as separable yet interacting motivational constructs revealed clinically meaningful profiles that were not captured by death-oriented ideation alone. DTL–DTD coupling emerged as a novel distinguishing feature, highlighting motivational profiling as a promising framework for understanding heterogeneity in adolescent suicide.
